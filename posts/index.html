Does the NBA Actually Have a Tanking Problem?

NBA
NBA_Draft
Tanking
Author

Tyler Ahlberg

Published

November 2, 2026

This past May, the National Basketball Association took an unprecedented step in how it handles its amateur draft. The NBA reformed its draft pick lottery system to directly punish the worst three performing teams with only a 5.4% chance at the first overall pick, while teams who finish 4-10 will have a 8.1% chance at the first overall pick, followed by positions 11-14 with a 5.4% chance and 14-16 with a 2.7% chance. This marks the first time one of the “big four” North American sports leagues didn’t outright reward the worst performing teams with the top pick or give them the most favorable odds in a lottery system for the amateur draft.

NBA commissioner Adam Silver would tell you the reason for these drastics changes are because of the practice of “tanking.” Tanking involves a team intentionally dropping games with the goal of securing a top pick in the amateur draft. A process that was modernized in the mid 2010s by former Philadelphia 76ers executive, Sam Hinkie, the man behind the infamous “Trust the Process” mantra. Though tanking has been present in the NBA for decades with disgraced ex-Los Angeles Clippers owner Donald Sterling being recorded on tape advocating to lose games for Virginia star Ralph Sampson. Now between the extended history of tanking and the recent lottery reforms one can begin to ponder, does the NBA actually have a tanking problem?

For this alleged technique of intentionally dropping games to truly be fruitful for those engaged, teams would have to be pretty confident in the level of difference maker a top pick represents.

Looking from the 2004 NBA Draft to the 2018 NBA Draft, or 15 total years of drafting one can see how slanted the level of talent is, relative to the draft position by win shares. Win shares is a statistic that combines stats that measure offensive contribution such as points and assists with stats that measure defensive contribution such as steals and opponent FG%. While not a perfect stat if you’re trying to discern the better of two players it does a good job showing which players have been a part of winning teams and have had meaningful contributions on those teams.

The NBA initially instituted the NBA Lottery system in 1985, where famed Georgetown center Patrick Ewing was selected 1st overall by the New York Knicks via the drawing of a (definitely not frozen) envelope. From 1985 to 2018, the lottery saw a pretty standard system of teams linearly having better odds the worst their record, bar minor adjustments in the early 90s. Specifically the worst team in the NBA having a 25% chance at the top pick and the remaining 75% distributed among the other 13 teams inclined towards the worst record.

This changed in 2019 after the aforementioned “Trust the Process” era where GM Sam Hinkie was very transparent about his “process” in something of an Al Capone style flaunting to the media. The change saw the worst three teams all have an equal 14% chance at the 1st overall pick, a large jump from the 25% for the sole worst team as was in the previous system. Followed by a continued flattening of the odds 4-14, with the fourth worst team having a 12.5% chance, the 5th with 10.5% and slowly decreasing from that. This change was meant to deter teams from tanking via a lottery system that didn’t allow the bottom teams to continuously secure premiere picks, but did this work?

In the eight years before the 2019 Lottery reform the worst team in the league didn’t receive less than a top two pick a single time. However, in the eight seasons post-reform the worst performing team only got a top two pick three times!

Mechanistically, the 2019 reform did its job, it hurt the value of tanking as you were no longer a shoe-in for a top two pick if you were the worst team in the league. Yet it asks the question, in spite of a rather hitting of the target by the NBA with the 2019 lottery reform, did the reform actually deter teams from outright tanking?

The answer is, sort of?

Looking at four years in the pre-reform seasons and four years in the post-reform seasons, after their initial bottom-five both groups saw the same amount of teams still remaining in the bottom five after two seasons with six. In the third and final tracked year in the bottom 5 we see the post-reform group actually have less teams hanging around at the bottom than the pre-reform group.

So it seems that the initial lottery overhaul to deter tanking was effective in its ability to steer bad teams away from getting what was basically a guaranteed top 2 pick while simultaneously keeping teams from sticking around the bottom of the pile at a better rate than the years before the reform. Yet we sit here months after the most drastic anti-tanking measure in the history of North American sports. Why? Does the NBA have a tanking problem? It doesn’t seem like it, the measures taken to deter tanking seem to have worked at comparable if not better rather than before the 2019 lottery change implementation.

Now we sit here in early 2026, the Sacramento Kings sit at a measly 1-10 record and are set to be punished for it with worse odds at acquiring top talent after a previous lottery reform seemingly worked? In the modern NBA bad teams are set to be punished for the first time in the history of the league, discontinuing a staple of not only the NBA, but North American sports at large. The attempted installment of parity via an even disreputation of the top amateur talent every season. The NBA has created a purgatory for bad teams.
